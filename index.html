about

Prom Night is one person. I write the words; a machine sings them. That's the whole arrangement, and I'd rather say it at the top than have you find it out sideways.

I'm a lawyer in St. Petersburg and have been for seventeen years, which is long enough to know what a room sounds like when somebody is finally telling the truth, and long enough to know how seldom that happens. Before that, campaigns. Before that, a political science degree I have put to almost no honest use.

The poems came first and they came slowly. Legal pads, the Notes app, the ten minutes before a hearing when there is nothing to do but wait. Most of them belong to a manuscript called Watch Me for the Changes, and none of them were supposed to leave the page. Then I found out you could hand a poem to a machine and get a song back.

Here is exactly what that means, since people ask and since it's the only part worth being clear about. Every word is mine — the lines, the images, the choruses, the order they arrive in. What the machine does is sing, and arrange, and play instruments that don't exist in a room that isn't there. It's a very good session player with no ideas of its own. When it offers me a chorus I throw the chorus away. When it sings a line I wrote, it sometimes finds a thing in the line I didn't know was in there.

The singer is called Sunoyama. Yokozuna, undefeated, does not exist.

I don't think this is the same as a band. I don't think it's nothing, either. The real answer is somewhere between those and I'm not going to pretend I've got it. What I've got is sixteen songs, another twenty on the bench, and every word in them meant.
